Product Design

Veevart's POS

Complete redesign of a Point of Sale for day to day activities in cultural institutions and non-profits.

Role
Product Designer
Team
Product, Customer Success & Engineering
Timeline
6 months
My focus
Modernizing the interface, flows and systems behind a multi-platform, modern POS
01

At a glance

Problem
The existing POS was slow, hard to scale, not multi-platform, and difficult to use day to day.
Insight
Staff couldn't find features that already existed, task-time data showed real friction, and engineering had to rebuild the same feature twice — once for desktop, once for iPad.
Impact
Staff testing showed noticeably faster ticket and membership sales.
↗ +35% faster sales
02

Context — why it matters

The POS is where a museum's day-to-day actually happens: ticket sales, memberships, donations, souvenirs, check-ins. When it works well, it's not just staff who benefit — supporters get their tickets and memberships faster, with clearer information, right at the front desk.

03

The problem, with evidence

Recorded task times showed staff were about 30% slower than expected when selling items. Over 45 support cases came in from more than 35 museums describing the same friction. Beyond speed, the interface itself worked against the people using it day to day: dense product grids with little breathing room, low-contrast secondary text, and icon-only controls with no visible label — real obstacles for a staff group that skews older and less tech-fluent.

Before — the previous POS interface.
04

Research — who we designed for

Research

Museum front-desk staff

Often volunteers, frequently older adults with basic technology literacy. Limited familiarity with advanced features. Some degree of limited vision or dexterity is common.

05

Design principles

Accessibility
Design for the staff we actually met in research — not just the average user.
Usability
Every feature has to be findable in the moment a visitor is waiting at the counter.
Consistency
One system, one set of patterns — whether staff are on an iPad or a desktop.
06

Key decisions

  1. 1

    A system where type size scales, built for staff who struggle to read small text.

  2. 2

    A design that adapts easily to each museum's own branding.

  3. 3

    A redesign that doesn't just modernize the previous system's functions, but genuinely improves them.

07

The flow — final screens

The prototype below is live — try it. It's embedded at a fixed width so the sidebar stays icon-only and Quick access shows its compact 2-column layout, matching how it looks on a smaller screen.

app.veevart.com/pos
08

Results

The redesign replaced a fragmented, hard-to-learn system with one built around how staff actually work at the counter — with measurable gains in speed and adoption. Ticket and membership sales got 35% faster, staff satisfaction rose 33.8% relative to the previous POS, and use of previously hard-to-find features — membership renewals, tax exemptions, per-item discounts — grew 40%.

Unifying Veevart's design system

Merging 5 legacy platforms into one product ecosystem, powered by a shared design system.

Role
Lead UX Designer
Team
Product, PMs & Engineering
Timeline
4 months
My focus
Unifying 5 legacy Veevart platforms into one ecosystem through a shared design system
01

At a glance

Problem
Veevart's legacy platforms were outdated and inconsistent in their components and flows across all products.
Insight
Veevart lacked a design system that simplified and gave direction to its products.
Impact
One ecosystem instead of five platforms, with 40% higher adoption and 15% less onboarding time, delivered in 4 months.
02

Context — why it matters

Led the end-to-end UX/UI design of the design system, focused on making components easy to use across multiple platforms. Worked closely with developers and PMs to understand each product's needs. The company was carrying technical debt, facing pressure to match competitors' quality, and needed to reinvent itself to satisfy investors.

03

The problem, with evidence

Veevart was seeing churn at double the expected rate — more than half of those cases tied to confusing flows and the fact that the same task worked differently depending on which part of the platform you were in. Over 20 clients raised leaving for simpler competitor platforms, citing both the confusing flows and how visually dated Veevart felt in comparison.

04

Research

Research methods

Conducted interviews and surveys with museum staff, benchmarking, and usage analysis, alongside conversations with investors about their vision for the product.

Interviews & surveys
25+ employees across 15 museums, plus every member of Veevart's implementation and customer success teams (20+ people).
Benchmarking
Compared Veevart's flows against the competitor platforms customers were considering switching to.
Usage analysis
Reviewed how staff actually completed tasks across the 5 legacy platforms — not just how they were designed to.
Stakeholder alignment
Conversations with investors about the vision and goals they needed the platform to support.

Who we designed for

Research

Museum staff

Front-desk and back-office museum employees who rely on Veevart daily to manage ticketing, memberships, and donations — often switching between multiple modules to complete a single task.

Research

Veevart team

Implementation, customer success, and product teams who support museums through onboarding and daily use, and who felt the inconsistency firsthand every time they had to explain the same action done differently in different modules.

05

Design principles

Reusability & modularity
Every component built once, then reused across all five platforms — instead of five different ways to solve the same interaction.
Flexibility & adaptability
A system flexible enough to serve very different product needs, without forking into five separate design languages.
Clarity & simplicity
Fewer ways to do the same thing, so staff and customers alike spend less time relearning how something works.
Consistency
One set of patterns, whether you're in ticketing, memberships, or donations — so switching modules doesn't feel like switching products.
06

Key decisions

  1. 1

    Some development teams had to rebuild their flows based on a pattern another team had already solved better — a hard trade-off that meant giving up ownership of a flow for the sake of consistency.

  2. 2

    Getting stakeholders to understand this would be a long process, requiring significant customer success effort to guide clients through the new platform.

07

The flow — final screens

Different modules of the same system — donor management, memberships, subscriptions — now share the same visual language and the same components.

08

Results

Bringing five platforms under one shared design system didn't just make the product look more consistent — it changed how quickly teams could ship, and how confidently customers could use what they built. Adoption grew 40% and onboarding time dropped 15% within 4 months of launch.

Veevart's online apps redesign

Redesigning Veevart's customer-facing pages — ticketing, donations, and membership signup — for the people using them, not just the staff behind them.

Role
Lead UX Designer
Team
Product, PMs & Engineering
Timeline
5 months
My focus
Redesigning Veevart's end-user pages for accessibility and ease of use across a wide range of visitors
01

At a glance

Problem
Veevart's customer-facing pages were outdated, unattractive, and had real usability problems — and their aging codebase made it hard to add the functionality customers were asking for.
Insight
The average museum visitor was a student or a parent — very different kinds of users — so the platform needed to be accessible, visually appealing, and easy to use for the widest possible range of people.
Impact
Positive reception from museums so far; over 160 clients will begin rolling these pages out to their thousands of customers over the coming year.
02

Context — why it matters

Led the end-to-end UX/UI design of Veevart's customer-facing pages, working with the same product, PM, and engineering teams as the design system project. The focus shifted from internal staff tools to the pages thousands of museum visitors interact with directly.

03

The problem, with evidence

Ticketing
Before — outdated layout, mixed languages, and a broken image placeholder on a live product page.
Membership
Before — a dense, multi-step flow with inconsistent copy and dated visual design.
04

Research

Research methods

Conducted interviews and surveys with end users, benchmarking, and usage analysis, alongside conversations with investors about their vision for the product.

Interviews & surveys
100+ end users surveyed and interviewed across 15 museums.
Benchmarking
Compared Veevart's flows against the competitor platforms customers were considering switching to.
Usage analysis
Reviewed how staff actually completed tasks across the 5 legacy platforms — not just how they were designed to.
Stakeholder alignment
Conversations with investors about the vision and goals they needed the platform to support.

Who we designed for

Research

Museum visitors

Students, parents, and casual visitors browsing exhibitions, buying tickets, or joining a membership online — often for the first time, with no prior familiarity with Veevart's system.

05

Design principles

Accessibility
Designed for the widest possible range of visitors — students, parents, and everyone in between — not just tech-savvy power users.
Control & feedback
Every action, from adding a ticket to submitting payment, gives clear, immediate feedback so people always know what happened.
Visual hierarchy
The most important action on each screen — buying, joining, paying — is always the most obvious one.
User focus
Built around how real visitors actually browse and buy, not around what was easiest to build.
06

Key decisions

  1. 1

    Some teams had to redesign their customer-facing flows around a pattern that had already proven itself elsewhere in the system, rather than building something new from scratch.

  2. 2

    Getting stakeholders on board meant accepting that this would take time — museums would need real support helping their customers adjust to the new experience.
